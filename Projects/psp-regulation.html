Overview

The payments world is undergoing an accelerated digital transformation. The center of gravity is shifting from traditional banks operating batch clearing systems to non-bank technology players processing billions of real-time transactions. Driven by the spread of digital wallets and open banking reforms, this shift created a dangerous regulatory vacuum: entities managing customer funds and collecting sensitive financial data were operating without a banking license and outside supervisory frameworks.

This review maps the regulatory frameworks that emerged around digital Payment Service Providers (PSPs) in seven markets, and evaluates each model against three criteria: financial stability, competition, and consumer protection & privacy.

The central conclusion: modern regulation is no longer just about enforcement and risk prevention — it has become a strategic tool that shapes market boundaries and creates new business models built on open data, algorithmic fairness and personalized services.

Three Layers of Payment Providers

The analysis distinguishes between three layers of activity in the payments ecosystem — each attracting a different regulatory focus.

Layer 1

Payment Gateways & Infrastructure

Provide the secure technological pipeline between merchants and acquirers via flexible APIs. They do not hold customer funds, so supervision focuses on operational and cyber security rather than prudential stability.

e.g. Stripe, Adyen, Tranzila

Layer 2

E-Money Issuers & Wallets

Convert fiat money into digital value held in virtual accounts. Because they hold customer funds without deposit insurance, they face capital requirements and strict safeguarding / segregation of client funds.

e.g. PayPal, Revolut, Bit, PayBox

Layer 3

Payment Initiation & Account Information

Born out of open banking: initiate account-to-account transfers and aggregate financial data across banks. Supervision centers on privacy, informed consent and data protection.

e.g. Plaid, TrueLayer, RiseUp

The Global Rule-Setters

Payments cross borders effortlessly, while laws stop at them. To prevent regulatory arbitrage, international bodies set standards that national regulators then adopt into local law:

  • FATF

    Financial Action Task Force — 40 recommendations against money laundering and terror financing, including the "Travel Rule" requiring sender/recipient details to accompany every digital transaction. Compliance is a de-facto condition for participating in global trade.

  • BIS

    Bank for International Settlements & CPMI — principles for financial market infrastructures, ensuring clearing and settlement systems withstand economic shocks and liquidity failures.

  • FSB

    Financial Stability Board (G20) — a strategic roadmap to make cross-border payments faster, cheaper and more transparent across jurisdictions.

  • ISO

    Technical standards — ISO 20022 as the common language of payment systems worldwide, and PCI-DSS as the baseline security standard for anyone handling card data.

Seven Markets, Seven Approaches

European Union

Centralized · Consumer-first · EBA / ECB

The global pace-setter. PSD2 broke the banks' monopoly on customer data and launched open banking, paired with Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) and GDPR privacy constraints. MiCA and the EU AI Act extend the perimeter to crypto-assets and algorithmic fairness. The main challenge: uneven enforcement across 27 member states invites regulatory arbitrage.

United States

Decentralized · Enforcement-based · FinCEN / CFPB

No single federal payments law. Federal supervision targets financial crime (money-transmitter registration, AML) and consumer protection, while licensing happens state-by-state — a company serving all of the US needs up to 50 separate licenses. Highly innovative, but with the heaviest bureaucratic entry barriers of any market reviewed.

United Kingdom

Risk-based · Innovation-friendly · FCA

Pioneer of the regulatory sandbox and mandated open banking (the CMA forced the nine largest banks to open their data). Combines aggressive pro-innovation tools with draconian safeguarding of client funds — including personal liability for senior managers. Post-Brexit divergence from the EU adds a dual-compliance burden.

Israel

Reform-driven · Pro-competition · BoI / ISA

In the middle of a deep structural reform adopting EU standards: the Payment Services Law (2019), the Financial Information Services Law (2021, "open banking") and the 2023 licensing law that moved supervision of payment companies to the Israel Securities Authority. The Bank of Israel opened direct access to national payment rails (Shva, Masav) for non-bank players.

Singapore

Risk-based · Tech-neutral · MAS

A single regulator (MAS) and a single Payment Services Act — technology-neutral, with license tiers scaled to activity volume, covering wallets, remittances and crypto under one roof. Sandboxes and open national infrastructure made it Asia's fintech hub, backed by some of the world's strictest AML and cyber requirements.

Australia & Canada

Modernizing · Data-rights focused · RBA / ASIC / BoC

Both fight concentrated banking oligopolies. Australia's Consumer Data Right extends open banking into a full open-data economy, giving consumers ownership of their financial data. Canada's Retail Payment Activities framework put fintechs under Bank of Canada supervision with mandatory segregation of client funds, though its open-banking rollout is slower.

Key Findings

  1. 1 · There is no single "right" model

    Regulatory design follows each country's financial history, market structure and strategic ambitions. The EU, UK, Singapore and Israel use directed regulation — actively engineering market structure through mandated open banking — while the US relies on after-the-fact enforcement, trading legal certainty for organic adaptability.

  2. 2 · Fund segregation replaced deposit insurance

    Since PSP failures (e.g. Wirecard, 2020) are not covered by classic deposit insurance, leading regimes require strict legal and physical segregation of client funds. Consumer protection is also expanding into digital rights: algorithmic fairness, transparency of automated decisions, and prevention of systemic bias in ML-based underwriting.

  3. 3 · Regulation is a catalyst, not only a barrier

    Regulatory sandboxes (UK, Singapore) bridge the gap between fast innovation and slow legislation. Open banking lowers entry barriers and fuels new services — while heavy compliance requirements paradoxically strengthen large incumbents and create a whole new RegTech market for automated KYC, fraud detection and transaction monitoring.

Conclusion

The central challenge facing regulators in the coming years is preserving the delicate balance between protecting the stability of the financial system and not strangling technological innovation. As the digital economy expands, international coordination, unified standards and flexible tools such as regulatory sandboxes will be critical to the continued growth of payment providers — and to public trust in modern financial systems.
